One delivery, one regulatory update

This executable walkthrough adds an individual drone to a bounded delivery scenario. It implements mission state transitions, elapsed time, battery consumption, and exposure accounting. It is a browser-based prototype, not a Mesa implementation or an empirically calibrated model.

The interactive figure follows request R1 through route assignment, outbound travel, delivery, and return. At the review boundary, the regulator calculates the next period's budgets.

Figure 1. Event-by-event execution of one delivery in the synthetic nine-neighborhood network. Circles show neighborhood exposure, the diamond identifies drone D1, and squares identify the depot and customer. Exposure values include an assumed history of earlier flights in the same period. The final event displays next-period budgets.

Model assumptions

The request arrives at time zero for destination Z2. One drone starts at the depot with 100% battery. The central route has return distance 12 units, while upper and lower alternatives each have return distance 16 units. The operator chooses the shortest feasible route and breaks ties by corridor index. Each chosen route consists of eight equal movement segments across the outbound and return journey. Node spacing in the drawing is schematic and does not encode segment length.

Flight speed is 2 distance units per minute. Battery consumption is 2 percentage points per distance unit, with a required reserve of 20%. Delivery takes one minute and consumes no battery in this simplified example. The review period closes at minute 10. These assumptions make timing inspectable and do not calibrate the abstract periods in the earlier pilot to real operating data.

All neighborhoods have a current exposure budget of 40 proxy units. Starting period exposures for N1 through N9 are [27, 26, 25, 24, 39.2, 24, 22, 23, 24]. These values are assumed earlier activity, not exposure generated by R1. Complete return-trip increments remain [1.1, 0.8, 1.0, 1.0, 1.4, 1.1, 0.9, 1.0, 0.8] along the chosen corridor only. Each neighborhood receives half its increment on the outbound crossing and half on the return crossing. Other route segments add no exposure in this simplified geometry.

Decisions and state updates

The operator rejects the central route because N5 would reach 40.6 units, exceeding its budget of 40. The upper route passes both the exposure and battery checks. The lower route is equally long and feasible, so the corridor-index rule chooses the upper route.

Before departure, the operator reserves the full return-trip exposure contribution. Each crossing transfers its contribution from reserved exposure to measured exposure. The reservation reaches zero at mission completion. This prevents the mission's return contribution from being omitted from its initial feasibility check. Concurrent missions are outside this example's scope.

Drone D1 reaches the customer at minute 4, completes delivery at minute 5, and returns to the depot at minute 9. Its battery is then 68%. The request becomes delivered at the customer, while the mission remains incomplete until the return leg finishes.

R1 adds 1.1 units to N1, 0.8 to N2, and 1.0 to N3. Their final period exposures are 28.1, 26.8, and 26.0. Other neighborhoods retain their assumed starting exposure.

At minute 10, the regulator uses the completed period's exposure. Information delay is zero for this walkthrough, so the observation affects the next period and never changes the budget for an already completed flight. The update is next_budget = clip(current_budget - 0.6 * (exposure - 28), 6, 70).

N1's next budget becomes 39.94. N2's becomes 40.72, and N3's becomes 41.20. N5's becomes 33.28 because of its earlier exposure, even though R1 avoided it. This shows why the regulator must respond to the entire period's exposure rather than attributing every adjustment to the selected mission.

The previous-event control replays recorded simulation states. It does not reverse the physical process. Next-event advances through states generated by the same transition rules. The display ends after calculating the next budgets, before any period 2 request arrives.

Discussion

The prototype now contains one operator's route choice, a drone with mission state, and a regulator's update. It still omits competing requests, recharging, fleet congestion, and measurement error. Battery consumption and exposure coefficients are assumed, so the run tests accounting and sequence rather than physical accuracy.

The next implementation should introduce a second request during the first mission. That would test drone availability and the remaining exposure reservations before expanding to a fleet.
